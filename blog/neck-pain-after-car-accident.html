Neck pain after a car accident: what's normal and when to get help

Plenty of people feel fine at the scene and then can't shoulder-check the next morning. Here's why that happens, what to watch for, and what actually helps.

By the Can-Am Physical Therapy teamNovember 21, 20265 min read

A rear-end collision at a stop light doesn't look like much. The bumper has a scuff, nobody is bleeding, and you drive home. Then you wake up the next day with a neck that won't turn more than halfway and a headache sitting at the base of your skull.

That delay is normal, and it's one of the most common things we hear from people who come to see us after a crash.

Why the pain often shows up a day later

In a collision your body is thrown forward and back faster than your neck muscles can react. The soft tissues around the joints of the neck get stretched past what they're used to. Whiplash is the everyday name for that kind of strain.

At the scene, adrenaline does a good job of hiding pain. Over the next several hours the strained tissue gets irritated and the muscles around it tighten up to protect the area. By the next morning (sometimes a day or two later) that guarding is what you feel as stiffness.

So "I felt fine right after" doesn't mean you weren't hurt.

Symptoms people commonly notice

Everyone's mix is a little different, but these come up a lot:

  • Neck pain and stiffness, especially turning your head or looking up
  • Headaches that start at the base of the skull
  • Pain spreading into the shoulders or between the shoulder blades
  • Dizziness or feeling off-balance
  • Feeling unusually tired, or having a hard time concentrating
  • Pins and needles running into the arms

Dizziness, trouble concentrating and headaches can also come from a concussion, which can happen even if you didn't hit your head. It's worth mentioning those symptoms at your assessment so they can be checked properly.

Go to an emergency department or call 911 if you have:

  • Numbness or weakness in your arms or legs
  • A severe headache, or one that keeps getting worse
  • Confusion, memory problems, or you were knocked out
  • Trouble speaking, swallowing or seeing
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control
  • Severe neck pain after a high-speed crash

What helps in the first few days

For most strains, gentle movement helps more than keeping the neck completely still. Turning your head slowly within a comfortable range, a few times an hour, keeps the joints from locking up. Long stretches in a soft collar usually aren't recommended unless a doctor has told you to wear one.

Heat or ice can take the edge off, so use whichever feels better. Sleep with a pillow that keeps your head level with your body rather than propped up high. If you work at a screen, get up every half hour or so.

What doesn't help is pushing through sharp pain to prove you're fine. Pain that stops you from sleeping, or that's getting worse instead of slowly settling, is a good reason to get assessed sooner. For medication questions, a pharmacist or your doctor is the right person to ask.

How physiotherapy fits in

At your first visit we take a full history of the accident and how you've felt since. Then we look at how your neck, upper back and shoulders move, check strength and sensation in your arms, and ask about headaches and dizziness. If something needs a doctor's attention, we'll tell you.

Treatment depends on what we find. It usually starts with hands-on work to ease stiff joints and tight muscles, along with simple exercises you can do at home to get your movement back. Where it suits you, techniques like dry needling or IASTM can help with stubborn muscle tension. As things improve, the exercises shift toward strength and the things you actually need to do again: shoulder-checking in the car, a full day at a desk, lifting your kids.

Recovery time varies a lot. Plenty of people feel a clear difference within a few weeks; others take longer, especially if headaches or dizziness are part of the picture. Getting started early tends to make the process easier.
